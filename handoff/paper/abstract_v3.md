# Abstract — v3 (3 September 2026; reflects the political-podcast frame)

## Full (~230 words)

Political podcasts are a major and under-measured political medium. We assemble a corpus of 204 U.S. political podcasts from the Apple Podcasts Politics chart (29,421 episodes; 1.38 million passages, 2018–2026) and measure how hosts address their listeners with published dictionaries and syntactic parsing. Show ideology labels agree with hosts' campaign-contribution scores (r = 0.68), expert ratings (κ = 0.75–0.77), and the party identification of each show's own listeners in a national survey (r = 0.91). Left- and right-leaning hosts differ not in hostility but in listener-directed address: right-leaning hosts speak to the audience in the second person and issue more instructions, a trait of the show that does not move with elections or topics. Linking the corpus to the Kettering–Gallup Democracy for All study (n ≈ 18,800) and the Pew American Trends Panel (n ≈ 9,000), people who get political news from podcasts score 0.22 SD higher on institutional cynicism than other news consumers after extensive controls, within both parties and at every education level, replicating in Pew with exposure measured months before the outcome. Among listeners of political podcasts inside the corpus and the six largest outside it, listener-directed address predicts a less formally educated audience (−0.27 SD per SD, income and age held constant) and, as an exploratory association, listeners who distrust elections and institutions (+0.27 and +0.22 SD per SD), net of populist and ideologically intense content. All audience results are associations under self-selection; the address–distrust association is pre-registered for Year 2.

## Structured (Objective / Methods / Results / Conclusion)

**Objective.** Political podcasts are a major news source whose content has rarely been measured at scale. We ask how left- and right-leaning political podcasts differ in the way hosts talk to their listeners, and who those listeners are.

**Methods.** We transcribed 204 U.S. political podcasts (29,421 episodes; 1.38 million passages, 2018–2026), measured hostility and listener-directed address (second-person speech and imperatives) with published dictionaries and syntactic parsing, and validated LLM-derived show ideology against hosts' campaign contributions, expert ratings, and the party of each show's own listeners. We linked the corpus to the Kettering–Gallup Democracy for All survey (n ≈ 18,800) and the Pew American Trends Panel (n ≈ 9,000), with small-sample cluster-robust inference for show-linked estimates.

**Results.** Ideology labels agree with contributions (r = 0.68), experts (κ = 0.75–0.77), and listeners' party (r = 0.91). Left and right differ not in hostility (r = 0.00 with ideology) but in listener-directed address (r = 0.39), a stable trait of the show. Podcast news consumers score 0.22 SD higher on institutional cynicism than other news consumers after extensive controls, in both parties and at every education level, with exposure preceding outcome in Pew. Within political podcasts inside and outside the corpus, listener-directed address predicts a less formally educated audience (−0.27 SD per SD, income held constant) and, exploratorily, listeners who distrust elections and institutions (+0.27 and +0.22 SD per SD), net of populist and ideologically intense content.

**Conclusion.** Partisan difference in political podcasting lies in how hosts address their audience, not in hostility, and that register determines who listens. Podcast audiences are distinctively cynical about institutions regardless of party or education. The study gives political communication a validated measure of mode of address and a population-level account of the podcast audience; the address–distrust link is pre-registered for confirmation.
