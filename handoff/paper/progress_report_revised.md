## Section 1: Work Completed

The project analyzes political podcast discourse along two representational arms that place different demands on the text. The register arm measures stylistic features and requires that the transcript remain intact, so advertising content is flagged rather than removed. The topic arm models thematic content and requires that advertising be excised, since sponsor copy is thematically coherent and would otherwise be recovered as topics. The two arms therefore differ in preprocessing, segmentation, and unit count, and the distinction is maintained throughout. All quantities below are labeled by arm where the arms diverge.

### 1.1 Corpus construction

The sampling frame is the Apple Podcasts US Politics subcategory chart, ranks 1 through 250, frozen on 13 July 2026. Two hundred and fifty is as deep as Apple serves a subcategory chart. Freezing the chart fixes the population against subsequent ranking changes and makes the frame reproducible from a single date.

A fixed-order inclusion filter reduced the 250 charted shows to 219. Fourteen shows were removed as outside US politics, identified by a publisher blocklist, a title-and-description regex, non-English feed language, and a curated exclusion list, applied in that order. Twelve were removed for holding under ten hours of available audio, a threshold set so that a show's profile rests on enough material to be stable. Five were removed for having no usable feed: two with no feed URL, two whose feed could not be reached, one whose feed could not be parsed. The filter was applied in fixed order so that each show's exclusion is attributable to a single stated reason, and the reason and evidence are recorded per show.

A further fifteen shows were dropped by a recorded decision on 14 August 2026 because they were not present in the stratified transcript sample used for the topic arm at the time that sample was fixed, leaving the analysis corpus of 204 shows. The decision record gives no pipeline failure as the cause, and none of the fifteen appears in the download-failure log; they were excluded to keep the two arms on the same show set rather than because a stage failed on them. The drop is not neutral with respect to the frame. The fifteen sit lower on the chart than the shows retained (median rank 192, interquartile range 146 to 234, against 117 and 56 to 180; Mann–Whitney p = .001), and they are disproportionately podcasts from institutional outlets — *The Economist*, *The Washington Post*, *The Atlantic*, NPR, the *Deseret News*, the *Minnesota Star Tribune* — along with *HasanAbi* and the Daily Wire's *Wired In*. The corpus is therefore slightly thinner at the chart tail and among legacy-outlet podcasts than the frame, and this should be stated as a scope condition.

The resulting corpus comprises 204 shows and 31,191 transcribed episodes, about 25,900 hours of audio, with a median episode of 47 minutes. *[The paper précis cites 29,421 episodes, the count at an earlier cutoff; the recovery run currently in progress will raise the figure again. Fix the number at the date you freeze the analysis and use it everywhere.]* Episodes are not full back catalogues and not a recency window. The sampler takes a census of every episode published before 1 January 2018, because that tail is thin and cannot be re-collected once feeds truncate, and then draws a fixed number of episodes per show per quarter from 2018 onward, ranked by a frozen hash so that larger draws nest earlier ones. Show-time is therefore aligned across daily and weekly shows, and a show's profile describes its output across the period rather than its most recent months. The bulk of the transcribed material falls between 2018 and mid-2026, with the pre-2018 census reaching back to 2010 for the oldest feeds.

### 1.2 Transcription

Audio was transcribed with the batched inference pipeline in faster-whisper, using the large-v3-turbo model in CTranslate2 format at float16 precision. Language was fixed to English rather than detected per file, which removes a source of segment-level failure on shows with multilingual clips. Voice activity detection used the pipeline's built-in Silero VAD. Speaker diarization was not performed, since both arms operate on show-level aggregates and do not require speaker attribution within an episode; its absence is a stated limitation for the second-person measure on interview-format shows, discussed in Section 3.

Inference ran at batch size 64 with one worker per GPU across two NVIDIA GB10 nodes, sustaining roughly 100 times real time; the corpus represents about 260 GPU-hours. *[Add calendar time if you want it; the runs were interleaved with other jobs and thermal throttling, so GPU-hours is the honest unit.]* Batch size was verified rather than assumed to affect only throughput: batch 128 produced transcripts identical to batch 64 (0.00% word difference) at 1.24 times the throughput, and batch 256 gave no further gain. Batch 64 was retained because a worker's unified-memory footprint rises from about 10 GB to 15 GB at 128. An int8-float16 variant was rejected because it changed 0.76% of words and was slower.

### 1.3 Advertising and boilerplate removal

The two arms handle advertising differently. Following Much et al. *[verify the citation: it is the source for the passage-level ideology instrument and for scoring the entire episode without sponsor removal]*, the register arm retains all passages and marks those carrying an ad marker — a regex over phrases such as "promo code," "sponsored by," "free trial," "go to [site].com" — so that stylistic measures are computed over uninterrupted text and any analysis can be re-run without flagged passages. The topic arm excises advertising and boilerplate spans at the sentence level. Detection used a pinned snapshot of gpt-4o-mini (2024-07-18) through the OpenAI Batch API with a single fixed prompt, over 131,463 sentence windows in the 7,626 episodes of the topic-arm sample; 51,675 raw spans were merged to 35,500 and excised, removing 5.9% of words (53.0 million to 49.9 million). The failure direction was fixed in advance: any window whose response was missing or malformed excised nothing, so errors leave advertising in rather than removing discourse. A cross-show boilerplate filter, which drops n-grams recurring across four or more shows, was rebuilt on the cleaned text.

Excision was validated before it was applied. A gold set of 886 sentences in 26 windows, one window from each of 26 shows, was sampled across five strata — host-read sponsor, network dynamic insert, show boilerplate, ambiguous product talk, and pure content — so that it contained advertising rather than the roughly 90% political content a uniform sample would return. Sentences were annotated as advertising, boilerplate, or content. The detector was required to clear a gate, set before evaluation, of at least .90 precision on advertising sentences and at most 2% false positives among the 538 content sentences. Observed content false positives were four sentences (0.7%), none substantive; recall was the weaker side, with 130 sentences missed. A cheaper model cleared the same gate with fewer false positives but missed 279 sentences and was not used. No excision was performed until the gate was cleared.

The gold set was annotated by an LLM agent rather than by a human annotator. This limits the validation: the annotator was a different model family from the detector, so the detector was not graded on its own output, but a bias the two share would be invisible to the gate. Every window is stored with its full text alongside the spans so that a human can audit or correct any judgment. Human re-annotation is planned before submission and is discussed in Section 4.

### 1.4 Segmentation

The register arm segments exhaustively into chunks of at most 750 characters, broken at sentence boundaries, with a sentence longer than the cap hard-split rather than dropped, producing 1.38 million chunks; register scoring then omits passages under 40 words. Exhaustiveness matters here because register measures are sensitive to systematic omission.

The topic arm segments the ad-excised text into passages of approximately 500 content words, with a minimum stub of 300 words so that short trailing segments are not modeled as documents in their own right, and with the same segmentation function used on the uncleaned text so that any difference is attributable to the excision. Segmentation of the uncleaned text yields 40,242 passages; after excision the topic arm operates on 37,942.

### 1.5 Topic model and selection of K

Topic models were fit with tomotopy over K in {20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 150}, with fixed settings (uniform term weighting, seed 0, 500 Gibbs iterations, c_v coherence on the top ten words). On the contaminated corpus and the 119 host-matched shows, held-out ideology R² rises from .21 at K = 20 through .33 at K = 50 to .36 at K = 75, reaches .41 at K = 100 and .38 at K = 150, with a permutation null near −.02 throughout. K = 75 was originally chosen as the first value in that plateau.

That original choice used the outcome, and leave-one-show-out cross-validation does not correct for it, because K was chosen with every show visible. The final analysis therefore re-derived K on the cleaned corpus by an outcome-independent criterion fixed before the sweep was inspected. Topic coherence peaks at K = 75 (c_v = .673, standard error .013 across topics), which is the justification the primary specification now rests on. The pre-specified conservative rule — the smallest K whose coherence lies within one standard error of the peak — selects K = 30, at which held-out ideology R² is .27. Both specifications are reported throughout. The two criteria disagree on K and on the magnitude of the recovered signal; they do not disagree on whether the signal exists, since R² = .27 at K = 30 remains far above a permutation null of about −.01. Ideology R² is measured at each K and reported, and it does not participate in choosing K.

### 1.6 Ground truth and target set construction

Ideology scores come from DIME CFscores (Bonica), 1979–2024 recipient and contributor files. Host names were extracted from RSS feed metadata in a fixed preference order — the feed's author field, then the owner name, then the RSS author element, then the show title — with each candidate classified as a person, an organization, or a repetition of the show title before it was accepted, because feeds routinely carry the network's name in the author field. Names were matched against DIME on surname plus first given name. Names with exactly one DIME candidate were resolved directly. Names with several were resolved in a fixed cascade: first by checking each candidate's DIME attributes (state, party, and seat for recipients; occupation and employer for contributors) against the episode titles, descriptions, and show context already in hand, and where that was insufficient, by manual web search under IRB approval, with every query and decision logged; names whose donor pool was too large to disambiguate were dropped as documented exclusions rather than guessed. The same cascade was applied to guests across 109,653 appearances: 16,043 resolved as a single recipient, 14,419 as a single donor, 25,689 by description, 9,346 by logged search; 11,182 remained ambiguous, 5,115 were dropped as too common, and 27,859 matched no donor at all, which is expected for journalists and pundits.

Among the 204 shows, 84 have full host coverage, 36 partial, 77 none, and 7 list no host. Three target sets were constructed, all against the same K = 75 topic vectors. *host_clean* covers the 84 shows whose hosts matched with full coverage, targets the mean host CFscore, and yields R² = .36 (r = .60). *extended_all* covers all 204 shows, using the host score where coverage is full and a guest-weighted mean CFscore otherwise, and yields R² = .43 (r = .66). *guest_uniform* assigns every show a guest-only score, covering the 202 shows with any DIME-matched guest, and yields R² = .61 (r = .79). Two robustness variants — restricting guest-primary rows to shows with at least twenty matched guests (188 shows, R² = .43) and inverse-variance weighting (R² = .43) — leave the headline unchanged.

*extended_all* is the primary specification despite *guest_uniform* producing the higher R². A guest-only score measures a show's booking behavior rather than the content of its host's speech, and shows that book overlapping guests inherit similar averaged CFscores whether or not their discourse resembles each other. The R² of .61 therefore reflects, in part, shared structure in the guest network rather than ideology recovered from discourse, which is the quantity the research question concerns. *guest_uniform* is retained as a robustness specification and its behavior is informative in its own right, but it does not answer RQ1 as posed.

### 1.7 Estimation

Ideology was predicted from centered log-ratio transformed show-level topic proportions using ridge regression. The feature scaler and the ridge penalty were both fit inside each cross-validation fold — the penalty by nested cross-validation over a small fixed grid — so that no information from the held-out show enters the fit. Cross-validation was leave-one-show-out, and every R² reported in this document is held out; in-sample R² was computed only to display the overfit gap and is never reported. A permutation null in which ideology scores are shuffled across shows returns R² between −.01 and −.03, confirming that the recovered signal is not an artifact of the estimation procedure.

The headline result is R² = .452 on the ad-cleaned corpus at K = 75 with n = 204 (*extended_all*). Three other figures appear in earlier materials and are reconciled here rather than left to differ. R² = .43 is the same target and K on the contaminated corpus, that is, before sponsor and boilerplate spans were excised; it is retained for the before-and-after comparison in Section 3. R² = .478 is the contaminated fit on the 119 host-matched shows, from the topic-PCA analysis. R² = .27 is the cleaned corpus at the coherence-selected K = 30. The lab presentation and any submitted text should carry .452 with its n and corpus, and name the others as alternative specifications when they appear.

### 1.8 Audience linkage *[include if the report's scope covers the August–September work; cut if it is confined to the two text arms]*

Since mid-August the corpus has been linked to two national surveys. Respondents to the Kettering–Gallup *Democracy for All* study (Year 1, n = 20,338) named up to three news sources in open text; 51,082 verbatims were matched to corpus shows by exact title (385 respondents, 51 shows), extended by a documented re-scan for misspellings and abbreviations (150 further respondents), and classified into news-diet segments for the full sample. The Pew American Trends Panel (Waves 150, 155, 165) supplies podcast-news frequency in mid-2024 and institutional trust two to eight months later. Show ideology labels were validated a fourth way against the party identification of each show's own listeners (r = .91 across 22 shows).

Two results from this arm are report-grade. Podcast news consumers score 0.22 SD higher on an eight-item institutional-cynicism scale than other news consumers with a full control set, within both parties and at every education level, and the association replicates in Pew with exposure measured before outcome. Within political podcasts, the register arm's listener-directed-address composite predicts a less formally educated audience (−0.27 SD per SD, income and age held constant), replicating across four samples. A third association — address with election distrust and cynicism among listeners — is reported as exploratory: a pre-registered out-of-frame test on ten large chart-absent shows failed as frozen, and the association holds only after the population is defined by political content (at least 15% political passages), a criterion adopted afterward, symmetric across corpus and out-of-frame shows, and insensitive to its threshold. The full pre-registration, its hashes, and the results are in the handoff record.

## Section 2: Observations and Expectations

The project began from the expectation that ideology would be the dominant axis of variation in political podcast discourse. *[Confirm this is what you actually expected before you saw the results; if your prior was weaker or different, say so, since the value of this section depends on the comparison being honest rather than tidy.]*

That expectation was not borne out at the primary specification. Ideology is recoverable from topic proportions at R² = .452, a substantial signal far above the permutation null, but at K = 75 it does not emerge as a leading component of variation. Three readouts from the same principal-component analysis of the centered-log-ratio topic profiles say this in different ways, all for the 204-show *extended_all* target (data/output/adspan/pca_diagnostics_table.csv; data/output/adspan/topic_pca_ideology_by_pc.csv). Among the seven components Horn's parallel analysis retains, the one most correlated with ideology is PC5 (|r| = .28, 5.1% of variance), with PC1 essentially uncorrelated (|r| = .03). Scanning every component rather than only the retained ones, the strongest single correlate is PC9 (|r| = .42 to .47, 2.6% of variance), a component the dimensionality test classifies as noise. And the supervised direction that best predicts ideology — the ridge axis — would rank fourteenth if inserted among the components by variance, carrying 1.5% of it. The placement is stable across the alternative transforms and across removing the register-heavy topics (PC5 in every such cell); it moves to PC3 on the 84 host-matched shows and to PC6 on the guest-only target. A parallel check on the raw show-by-word matrix, skipping the topic model, also finds ideology low-variance, so the topic step is not what compressed it. At K = 75, the corpus varies more along other dimensions than along ideology.

The qualification is K = 30, the coherence-rule specification. There, ideology's strongest correlate is PC2, which carries 18.7% of variance and is a dominant axis, though the correlation is weak (|r| = .15) and the supervised axis still ranks thirteenth (1.6% of variance). So "ideology is not a leading axis" is a claim about the finer topic representation; at the coarser one, ideology is entangled with the second-largest thematic axis rather than absent from the top of the scree. Both are reported.

Two readings of this result are available and they lead in different directions. The first treats it as measurement failure: ideology is present in the discourse, the representation is too coarse to isolate it, and the leading components capture noise or artifacts that a better model would suppress. The second treats the thematic structure as real and prior: shows are organized primarily by what they talk about, and ideology is expressed within those thematic commitments rather than across them.

The evidence favors the second reading. A representation too coarse to capture ideology would recover it weakly or not at all, and the leading components would resist substantive interpretation. Neither holds here. Ideology is recovered at a level well clear of the null, and it is recovered specifically from topic proportions rather than from stylistic features. The components that dominate are interpretable as thematic rather than residual. What the results describe is a corpus in which ideology is organized under thematic structure, which is a different claim from ideology being weakly present.

This is consistent with issue ownership. If parties and the media aligned with them compete by emphasizing the issues on which they hold an advantage rather than by taking opposing positions on a shared set of issues, then the primary observable difference between shows is which issues they raise, and ideological position is recoverable as a secondary structure organized within that emphasis. The pattern observed here matches that prediction.

Two further observations support this. First, the signal lives in content rather than in style, in the sense of variance recovered: a ridge model on full sentence-embedding vectors, the closest style-side analogue, recovers ideology at R² = .21 under the same held-out harness, against .33 to .45 for topics at matched sample sizes, and the register composite correlates r = .43 with host CFscore. Style does differ by side — listener-directed address separates left from right at d = 0.78 — but it explains less of the ideological gradient than what shows talk about. Second, the ordering across target sets is informative in itself: *host_clean* yields R² = .36, *extended_all* .43, and *guest_uniform* .61. The gradient is steeper than measurement error alone would explain and is consistent with guest booking carrying network structure that host speech does not, which is why *guest_uniform* is treated as a robustness check rather than the primary specification.

## Section 3: Problems Encountered

The problems fall into two classes. Infrastructure problems delayed the work without threatening its validity. Methodological problems threatened validity and required changes to the analysis.

### Infrastructure

The transcription pipeline was built on the lab's two GB10 nodes, which required establishing key-based SSH access, constructing a GPU environment on each, and resolving two library conflicts that prevented CTranslate2 from initializing. The first was a cuDNN import failure (`libcudnn.so.9` not found): the Python environment carried its own cuDNN wheel that the CTranslate2 loader could not see, resolved by pointing the loader at the wheel's library directory. The second was a version mismatch between the CTranslate2 Python package (4.8.1) and the shared library present on one node (4.7.1), which surfaced as an undefined-symbol error at import; it was resolved by installing the matching library from the other node. Both fixes are recorded in the setup notes so they do not have to be rediscovered when the pipeline is rebuilt.

An early run on rma2 produced a series of CUDA out-of-memory failures, recorded in the transcription log against specific episodes. The cause was memory pressure from running more than one transcription worker per GPU on long episodes, each worker holding about 10 GB of unified memory at batch 64. It was resolved by fixing one transcriber per GPU under a supervisor script that also throttles on GPU temperature, and by writing the failed episodes to a recovery manifest that is re-transcribed at the end. The batch-size comparison described in Section 1.2 confirmed that the memory-driven choice of batch 64 over 128 costs nothing in transcript quality. One node is shared with another user's GPU job, which halved its duty cycle for part of the run; the supervisor accommodates this rather than contending for the device.

Version control through git and a working Claude Code setup on the lab server were established early, which made the environment reproducible across the two nodes and limited the cost of these interruptions.

### Methodological

Four methodological problems arose, of which three are resolved.

The first was selection of K on the outcome variable. The plateau rule that originally selected K = 75 evaluated candidate models by held-out ideology R², which makes the R² reported at that value optimistic. The problem was identified after the primary specification had been fit. K was then re-derived on the cleaned corpus under a coherence criterion that never sees the outcome; coherence peaks at K = 75, which is now the stated justification, while the more conservative one-standard-error rule selects K = 30. Both are reported. Because the RQ1 conclusion survives under both, the disagreement between criteria is a bound on the estimate rather than a threat to it.

The second was contamination of the initial fit by advertising. In the K = 75 model fit on the uncleaned corpus, sponsor copy and show boilerplate — which are thematically coherent — formed topics of their own, identified by their top words hitting the advertising and boilerplate marker lists and the spoken-filler stoplist. Dropping those topics from the contaminated fit lowered held-out R² from .451 to .395, which showed that part of the original signal was carried by advertising that co-varies with ideology through sponsor choice. The corpus was cleaned by span excision (Section 1.3) and the model refit; the cleaned fit contains no contaminant topic and yields R² = .452. The earlier figure of .43 is retained only for comparison.

The third was ambiguity in matching hosts and guests to DIME records. Surname plus given name is not unique across a database of that size, and naive matching produced multiple candidate records for a substantial share of names. This was addressed with the fixed three-stage cascade described in Section 1.6, in which DIME attributes are checked against show and episode context before any manual resolution, every manual resolution is logged, and names that cannot be disambiguated are dropped rather than guessed. The logging is what makes the matching auditable rather than merely defensible.

The fourth is unresolved. The gold set used to validate advertising excision was annotated by an LLM agent rather than a human, so the detector was measured against a standard that may share its failure modes. The gate was cleared comfortably, but the gate is weaker evidence than it appears. Human re-annotation of the 886-sentence gold set is the first item in Section 4.

Two further limits are anticipated rather than encountered. The corpus is built from a chart frozen on a single date, which makes the frame reproducible but ties the sample to one moment in a fast-moving medium; whether the findings hold on a frame drawn at a different date is not something the current design can answer. And the second-person measure in the register arm cannot distinguish address to the listener from address to a guest on interview-format shows; the corpus frame contains few such shows and the partisan contrast survives removing the interrogative component, but the measure's level is not comparable across formats without diarization, which the archived audio would permit.

## Section 4: Remaining Goals

The immediate goal is human re-annotation of the advertising gold set. This is the one open threat to a result the topic arm depends on, and it is tractable at 886 sentences.

The second is reconciliation of the reported R² across documents. Four figures are in circulation (.43, .452, .478, and .27), each valid for its own specification, and each has appeared in some combination of the interim reports, the lab presentation, and the working analysis. The headline is fixed at .452 with its n and corpus, carried wherever it appears, with the others named as alternative specifications.

The third is human validation of the passage-level ideology labels. Show labels are validated four ways; individual passage labels have not been checked against human coders. A 200-passage, two-coder check with reported agreement precedes submission.

The fourth is the write-up of RQ3 and RQ4. *[Both are analytically complete but neither appears in the lab presentation, which covers RQ1 and RQ2. Confirm the numbering.]* They need to be brought to the same standard of documentation as the first two before the final paper.

The fifth is the reporting of corpus size in episodes and hours at the frozen date, and a one-sentence statement of the chart-tail and institutional-outlet skew among the fifteen dropped shows.

*[If Section 1.8 is in scope:]* The sixth is the Year 2 pre-registration for the audience arm, to be filed before the Year 2 data arrive: the political-content frame stated in advance, election distrust as the primary outcome for listener-directed address, populist vocabulary and ideological intensity as covariates, and a request for interview dates so that within-show variation in address can be used with show fixed effects.

The goals have changed over the course of the project in two respects. *[Confirm.]* The first is a narrowing within the text work, from four research questions pursued in parallel toward RQ1 and RQ2 as the load-bearing results, with effort shifted from producing new analyses toward robustness and provenance auditing of existing ones. The second is an extension outward: the linkage to survey data added an audience arm that the original design did not have, and the register arm, which began as a description of how the sides talk, now also describes who listens. The research questions themselves have not changed. What changed is the weight placed on establishing that the answers to the first two are sound, and the addition of an audience-side question that the corpus alone could not ask.
