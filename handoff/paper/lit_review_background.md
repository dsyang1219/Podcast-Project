# Background reading for *Talking at the Audience*

Grouped in the order a background section would use them: the medium, why it might matter (intimacy and
address), what earlier work measured instead (hostility), the outcome literature (trust, cynicism, election
confidence), the rival explanation (populism), and the measurement conventions the paper follows. Two sentences
each; the italic line says what the paper takes from it.

## 1. Podcasts as a political medium

**Pew Research Center (2023). *Podcasts as a Source of News and Information*.** About half of US adults had
listened to a podcast in the past year, and two-thirds of listeners had heard news discussed on one, but only one
in five listened to a podcast connected to a news organisation. Listening is dominated by entertainment,
learning and background-listening motives rather than staying informed.
*Establishes the scale of the medium and that political podcasts are mostly host-branded, not institutional.*

**Wirtschafter, V. (2023). *Audible Reckoning: How Top Political Podcasters Spread Unsubstantiated and False
Claims*. Brookings.** Analysed 36,603 episodes from 79 leading political podcasts and found roughly 70% of the
shows had aired at least one unsubstantiated or false claim, with election-fraud claims rising almost 600% after
the 2020 election, overwhelmingly on conservative shows. Also the source of the Brookings lean labels used to
validate the ideology axis.
*The closest prior corpus study of political podcasts; it studies content (claims) where this paper studies register.*

**Chan-Olmsted, S. & Wang, R. (2022). "Understanding podcast users: Consumption motives and behaviors."
*New Media & Society*.** Survey-based typology of why people listen and how (device, setting, multitasking).
Finds companionship and habit alongside information motives.
*Background for treating listening as a relationship with a host rather than exposure to a news product.*

**Perks, L. G. & Turner, J. S. (2019). "Podcasts and productivity: A qualitative uses and gratifications study."
*Mass Communication and Society* 22(1): 96–116.** Focus groups with regular listeners; identifies multitasking,
customisation and displacement of other media, and extends the companionship category to parasocial
relationship formation with hosts.
*Qualitative grounding for the parasocial mechanism.*

**Berry, R. (2006). "Will the iPod kill the radio star? Profiling podcasting as radio." *Convergence* 12(2).**
Early account of podcasting as a descendant of radio with a more personal, on-demand and unregulated form. Argues
the intimacy of the earpiece and the absence of gatekeepers are the medium's defining features.
*Historical framing: the register you measure is inherited from radio talk but freed from broadcast norms.*

**Spinelli, M. & Dann, L. (2019). *Podcasting: The Audio Media Revolution*. Bloomsbury.** Book-length treatment
of podcast form, production and audiences, with chapters on intimacy, the voice and the "conversational" style.
Positions podcasting as a distinct medium rather than radio online.
*General reference for the medium's conventions.*

## 2. Intimacy, parasocial relationships and direct address

**Horton, D. & Wohl, R. R. (1956). "Mass communication and para-social interaction." *Psychiatry* 19(3).** The
original statement that broadcast performers simulate face-to-face conversation with an absent audience, and that
audiences respond as if the relationship were real. Introduces the term "persona" for the host figure.
*The theoretical root of the address measure: talking to the listener is the performer's core technique.*

**Dibble, J. L., Hartmann, T. & Rosaen, S. F. (2016). "Parasocial interaction and parasocial relationship:
Conceptual clarification and a critical assessment of measures." *Human Communication Research* 42(1).**
Separates the momentary sense of being addressed during exposure (interaction) from the enduring bond
(relationship), and reviews how each is measured. Shows that the two are often conflated.
*Lets the paper say precisely which construct address plausibly drives: interaction in the moment.*

**Schlütz, D. & Hedder, I. (2022). "Aural parasocial relations: Host–listener relationships in podcasts."
*Journal of Radio & Audio Media* 29(2): 457–474.** Survey of 804 German podcast listeners; headphone listening,
conversational style and host self-disclosure strengthen parasocial relationships, which in turn predict attitude
and behaviour change. The first quantitative evidence that audio alone sustains persuasive parasocial bonds.
*Direct support for the causal story the paper proposes but could not confirm.*

**Euritt, A. (2023). *Podcasting as an Intimate Medium*. Routledge.** Argues that podcasting produces intimacy
as its characteristic product, traced from bodily voice through parasocial interaction to national imaginaries,
using close readings of American podcasts including political round-tables.
*The humanities account of the same phenomenon; useful for the framing paragraph.*

**Fairclough, N. (1989). *Language and Power*; (1995). *Media Discourse*.** Coins "synthetic personalisation":
mass media addressing each member of a mass audience as an individual ("you"), and describes the
"conversationalisation" of public discourse. Treats second-person address as a technique of power, not a
stylistic accident.
*The linguistic concept behind measuring second person and imperatives as a register.*

**Scannell, P. (1996). *Radio, Television and Modern Life*. Blackwell.** Broadcast talk is designed to be
overheard by absent listeners and is therefore built as sociable, direct address to them. Broadcasters' "for
anyone as someone" structure is what makes the medium feel personal.
*Explains why hosts talk at the audience at all.*

**Montgomery, M. (1986). "DJ talk." *Media, Culture & Society* 8(4).** Close analysis of radio DJ monologue,
showing how it simulates dialogue with the listener through direct address, imperatives and response tokens.
One of the earliest empirical descriptions of listener-directed talk.
*Shows the same features you count were the marks of intimate radio forty years ago.*

**Hutchby, I. (1996). *Confrontation Talk: Arguments, Asymmetries and Power on Talk Radio*. Erlbaum.**
Conversation-analytic study of British call-in radio, showing how hosts hold the power to define, challenge and
close. Establishes talk radio as a site where address and argument are the product.
*Bridges to the talk-radio politics literature below.*

**Orvell, A., Kross, E. & Gelman, S. A. (2017). "How 'you' makes meaning." *Science* 355(6331).** Generic
"you" is used to express norms and to distance the self from negative experience; it turns personal statements
into general rules. Experimental and corpus evidence across several studies.
*Why second person is not just a pronoun count: it carries normative force.*

**Cruz, R. E., Leonhardt, J. M. & Pezzuti, T. (2017). "Second person pronouns enhance consumer involvement and
brand attitude." *Journal of Interactive Marketing* 39: 104–116.** Field study of brand posts plus two experiments:
"you" increases involvement and attitude through self-referencing, for less collectivist consumers. The effect is
about being spoken to, not about the message content.
*Experimental evidence that address changes engagement independently of content.*

## 3. Talk radio, partisan media and the outrage literature

**Jamieson, K. H. & Cappella, J. N. (2008). *Echo Chamber: Rush Limbaugh and the Conservative Media
Establishment*. Oxford.** Documents how Limbaugh, Fox and the Wall Street Journal editorial page formed a
self-protective media enclave whose audiences distrust outside sources. Uses content analysis and panel surveys.
*The precedent for linking a host's talk to listeners' distrust of institutions and of mainstream media.*

**Barker, D. C. (2002). *Rushed to Judgment: Talk Radio, Persuasion, and American Political Behavior*. Columbia.**
Panel evidence that talk-radio exposure moved opinions on issues the hosts emphasised, beyond selection.
Argues persuasion comes from the host relationship and repeated exposure.
*The strongest earlier causal claim for a host-driven audio medium.*

**Sobieraj, S. & Berry, J. M. (2011). "From incivility to outrage: Political discourse in blogs, talk radio, and
cable news." *Political Communication* 28(1).** Defines and counts thirteen forms of "outrage" speech across
media, finding it pervasive and more common on the right. Their codebook is the basis of the outrage lexicon in
this paper.
*The hostility measures that turn out not to separate left from right in the podcast corpus.*

**Berry, J. M. & Sobieraj, S. (2014). *The Outrage Industry: Political Opinion Media and the New Incivility*.
Oxford.** Extends the 2011 article into an account of outrage as a business model, with audience interviews
describing why people enjoy it.
*Context for the null on hostility: outrage is real but not what distinguishes political podcasts.*

**Mutz, D. C. (2015). *In-Your-Face Politics: The Consequences of Uncivil Media*. Princeton.** Experiments
showing that close-up, uncivil televised conflict raises arousal and memory but lowers trust in politicians and
regard for the other side.
*Establishes the trust consequence of hostile style, against which address is a different mechanism.*

**Young, D. G. (2020). *Irony and Outrage: The Polarized Landscape of Rage, Fear, and Laughter in the United
States*. Oxford.** Argues that satire and outrage are the two genre homes of the left and right, rooted in
psychological differences. Genre, not just content, sorts audiences.
*Supports the paper's claim that left and right differ in register more than in hostility.*

**Rathje, S., Van Bavel, J. J. & van der Linden, S. (2021). "Out-group animosity drives engagement on social
media." *PNAS* 118(26).** Posts about the political out-group are shared far more than in-group posts across
millions of social-media messages. Out-group language, not negativity per se, predicts virality.
*Source of the out-group-reference measure; a hostility construct that did not carry the audience result.*

**Brady, W. J. et al. (2017). "Emotion shapes the diffusion of moralized content in social networks." *PNAS*
114(28).** Each moral-emotional word in a tweet raised retweets by about 20%, mostly within ideological groups.
*Source of the moral-emotional lexicon in the style battery.*

**Prior, M. (2007). *Post-Broadcast Democracy*. Cambridge.** Media choice lets the politically interested consume
more and the uninterested opt out, widening gaps in knowledge and turnout. Effects of media depend on who
selects into them.
*The selection problem the paper's design (survey linkage, not experiment) cannot escape and says so.*

**Arceneaux, K. & Johnson, M. (2013). *Changing Minds or Changing Channels? Partisan News in an Age of Choice*.
Chicago.** Experiments with free choice show partisan media effects concentrated among the small audience that
seeks them out. Forced-exposure designs overstate media influence.
*Why podcast audiences (self-selected, small, intense) are exactly where composition and effect are hard to separate.*

**Levendusky, M. (2013). *How Partisan Media Polarize America*. Chicago.** Partisan media polarise mainly their
existing viewers, making them more extreme and less trusting of the other side. Small audience, large per-capita
effect.
*Parallel to the paper's finding that effects are visible only inside the political-content frame.*

**Guess, A. M. (2021). "(Almost) everything in moderation: New evidence on Americans' online media diets."
*American Journal of Political Science* 65(4).** Web-tracking data show most Americans' news diets are moderate
and overlapping; a small tail consumes highly partisan sources.
*Frames the podcast-diet segment as a minority, and motivates the "exclusive diet" outcome.*

## 4. Trust, cynicism and election confidence

**Cappella, J. N. & Jamieson, K. H. (1997). *Spiral of Cynicism: The Press and the Public Good*. Oxford.**
Strategy-framed coverage of politics breeds cynicism about politicians and institutions, which in turn rewards
more strategic coverage. Experimental and survey evidence.
*The founding statement of media-driven institutional cynicism, the paper's main outcome.*

**de Vreese, C. H. (2005). "The spiral of cynicism reconsidered." *European Journal of Communication* 20(3).**
Replicates and qualifies the spiral: strategy news raises cynicism, but effects are conditional and cynicism does
not depress turnout among the engaged.
*Supports the "engaged anti-institutionalism" reading of the podcast-diet profile.*

**Aalberg, T., Strömbäck, J. & de Vreese, C. H. (2012). "The framing of politics as strategy and game: A review of
concepts, operationalizations and key findings." *Journalism* 13(2).** Systematises the strategy/game frame and
reviews its measured effects on cynicism.
*Source of the game-frame lexicon used as a competing content explanation.*

**Ladd, J. M. (2012). *Why Americans Hate the Media and How It Matters*. Princeton.** Traces declining trust in
the press to elite criticism and market fragmentation, and shows that distrust of media makes people rely on
partisan cues instead.
*Background for the exclusivity outcome (naming no institutional news source).*

**Hetherington, M. J. (2005). *Why Trust Matters*. Princeton; Citrin, J. & Stoker, L. (2018). "Political trust in
a cynical age." *Annual Review of Political Science* 21.** Together, the standard account of what political trust
is, why it fell, and what it predicts (support for government action, compliance, polarisation). The review
distinguishes cynicism from healthy scepticism.
*Defines and situates the 8-item cynicism composite.*

**Berlinski, N. et al. (2023). "The effects of unsubstantiated claims of voter fraud on confidence in elections."
*Journal of Experimental Political Science* 10(1): 34–49.** Survey experiment after the 2018 midterms: exposure
to fraud claims lowered confidence in electoral integrity, especially when congenial, and fact-checks did not
repair it.
*Election distrust as a movable outcome; motivates the Year-2 primary outcome.*

**Clayton, K. et al. (2021). "Elite rhetoric can undermine democratic norms." *PNAS* 118(23).** Exposure to
Trump's attacks on the 2020 election reduced trust in elections and support for democratic norms among his
supporters.
*Complements Berlinski: rhetoric, not only claims, moves election confidence.*

**Zaller, J. R. (1992). *The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion*. Cambridge.** Reception and acceptance of
messages depend on political awareness; less-aware citizens accept more of what they receive but receive less.
*Theory behind expecting, and testing, education differences in who address-heavy shows reach.*

## 5. Populism as the rival explanation

**Mudde, C. (2004). "The populist Zeitgeist." *Government and Opposition* 39(4).** Defines populism as a thin
ideology dividing society into a pure people and a corrupt elite. The definition behind every dictionary measure.
*What "anti-elite" means when the paper tests populism against address.*

**Rooduijn, M. & Pauwels, T. (2011). "Measuring populism: Comparing two methods of content analysis." *West
European Politics* 34(6).** Compares human coding with a dictionary of people-centric and anti-elite terms, and
finds the dictionary recovers the same party ordering.
*Source of the populism dictionary; the paper finds populism and address are not separable within the corpus.*

**Krämer, B. (2014). "Media populism: A conceptual clarification and some theses on its effects." *Communication
Theory* 24(1).** Distinguishes populism in the media from populism by the media, and proposes that a populist
style of address (speaking for "the people", anti-elite, emotional) has effects of its own.
*The closest existing concept to "register" in the populism literature.*

**Fawzi, N. (2019). "Untrustworthy news and the media as 'enemy of the people?'" *International Journal of
Press/Politics* 24(2): 146–164.** German survey: anti-elite populist attitudes predict lower media trust and
satisfaction across the board; people-centrism does not.
*Source of the anti-media reference measure and reason to expect populism to predict exclusivity.*

## 6. Measurement and inference conventions

**Biber, D. (1988). *Variation across Speech and Writing*. Cambridge.** Factor analysis of linguistic features
yields dimensions of register (involved vs informational, narrative, etc.), with second person, contractions and
hedges loading on the "involved" pole.
*The tradition the term "register" and the feature battery come from.*

**Much et al. (2026).** The passage-level ideology instrument the corpus uses: a two-stage LLM rubric (is it
political; direction on a 7-point scale) validated at 750-character speaker turns with kappa 0.834 against human
coders. *Cited in `step4_ideology/score_ideology.py`; check the full reference before submission.*

**Gilardi, F., Alizadeh, M. & Kubli, M. (2023). "ChatGPT outperforms crowd-workers for text-annotation tasks."
*PNAS* 120(30).** LLM zero-shot labels matched or beat crowd workers on relevance, stance and frame tasks at a
fraction of the cost. Argues for LLMs as annotators with human validation.
*Justifies LLM labelling with a human validation set.*

**Ziems, C. et al. (2024). "Can large language models transform computational social science?" *Computational
Linguistics* 50(1).** Benchmarks LLMs across 25 social-science annotation tasks; good on many, unreliable on
some, so they should augment rather than replace coders.
*The cautionary citation to pair with Gilardi.*

**Pustejovsky, J. E. & Tipton, E. (2018). "Small-sample methods for cluster-robust variance estimation and
hypothesis testing in fixed effects models." *Journal of Business & Economic Statistics* 36(4).** CR2
(bias-reduced) standard errors with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom keep test size correct with few clusters.
*The primary inference used for every show-clustered estimate.*

**Cameron, A. C., Gelbach, J. B. & Miller, D. L. (2008). "Bootstrap-based improvements for inference with
clustered errors." *Review of Economics and Statistics* 90(3).** The wild cluster bootstrap gives reliable
p-values with as few as five to ten clusters.
*The second inference reported alongside CR2.*

**Nosek, B. A. et al. (2018). "The preregistration revolution." *PNAS* 115(11).** Distinguishes prediction from
postdiction and argues that pre-specifying tests is what makes confirmatory claims credible.
*Frames why three failed pre-registered tests are reported as results and the frame finding as exploratory.*
