# Discussion (v2, 15 September 2026)

## What the study adds

The outrage literature taught us to look for the difference between left and right opinion media in tone: in
insult, mockery, catastrophising and the naming of enemies (Sobieraj & Berry 2011; Berry & Sobieraj 2014). In the
political podcast chart that expectation fails. Across 194 shows the hostility measures do not sort the sides
at all; what sorts them is the grammar of address. Right-leaning shows talk *at* the listener, with imperatives
and second person, at rates high enough to place a show on the correct side four times in five from that
alone. This is the study's most secure result, because it does not depend on the survey, it replicates across a
parse-based and a dictionary-based instrument and, on a subsample, across an independent published tagger, and
it survives every decomposition we tried. It also fits what the broadcast-talk tradition would predict rather
than what the outrage tradition would: the podcast is the descendant of the intimate radio monologue
(Montgomery 1986; Scannell 1996), and its characteristic move is synthetic personalisation, the mass audience
addressed one listener at a time (Fairclough 1989), not the shouting match (Mutz 2015).

The second result is that this is a property of shows rather than of moments. Address does not move when the news
does, across eight shocks and their placebos, and it does not move with topic. That matters for interpretation:
whatever address does to or for an audience, it does so as a standing feature of the relationship, which is the
condition under which parasocial bonds form (Horton & Wohl 1956; Dibble, Hartmann & Rosaen 2016) and, on the
recent audio evidence, persuade (Schlütz & Hedder 2022).

## What the audience data do and do not show

Listeners who name address-heavy shows as a news source are more distrustful of elections, more cynical about
institutions, less educated and more likely to name no institutional news source at all. Each of these holds in
the full corpus sample and inside the political-content frame, at every reasonable cut-off, with any single show
removed, and net of party, attention, age, education, platform use and the show's lean. None of it is explained
by populist vocabulary, which was the obvious rival (Mudde 2004; Krämer 2014; Rooduijn & Pauwels 2011): populism
adds nothing to the models and predicts nothing on its own.

But the design cannot say that address is what carries these associations, for two reasons that should be
stated as plainly as the associations themselves.

First, the three pre-registered tests that would have supported a directional claim all failed, one of them on
exactly this population of corpus listeners. The exploratory associations were found afterwards, in a larger
sample and with a frame defined afterwards. By the standard we committed to in advance (Nosek et al. 2018), they
are hypotheses for Year 2, not findings of Year 1, and we report them in that spirit.

Second, address-heavy shows are also ideologically intense shows (r = 0.44), and intensity predicts every
outcome at least as well. Net of intensity, address keeps a marginal coefficient on cynicism and nothing else.
Three readings are available and the data cannot choose among them. Intensity may be a confounder: one-sided
content attracts distrustful, less-educated audiences, and such content happens to be delivered in the
imperative. Intensity and address may be two faces of one register, the sermon, in which case partialling one
from the other asks a question the corpus cannot answer. Or intensity may be partly an artefact of address: the
labelling model may read a passage that tells the listener what to think as more one-sided than the same claim
stated flatly, which would make intensity a second, noisier measurement of address rather than a rival. The
within-show check that separates the third reading from the first two (do a show's own address-heavy passages
receive more non-moderate labels?) is straightforward and is queued; until it is done, the honest summary is that
listeners of address-heavy, ideologically intense political podcasts differ from other listeners in a coherent
anti-institutional direction, and that the corpus of 54 matched shows is too small to apportion that between the
two properties.

Underneath both is the limit every observational media study shares (Prior 2007; Arceneaux & Johnson 2013): the
audiences of these shows are self-selected, and the same associations arise if distrustful people seek out hosts
who talk to them as if influence ran the other way. The education result is the clearest instance. It is the
most stable association in the data, and it is entirely compatible with less-educated listeners preferring shows
that address them directly, a preference the parasocial literature would predict and the podcast-use literature
describes (Perks & Turner 2019; Chan-Olmsted & Wang 2022). We report it as audience composition, which is what
it demonstrably is.

## Limitations

The corpus is one country's most popular political podcasts in one summer, sampled by a ladder that favours breadth
across shows over depth within them; 194 of 219 eligible shows carry the measures of record, and ten shows that
had too few transcripts when the show table was fixed remain to be scored against the frozen reference. The
address measure's dictionary component, and all of the hostility batteries, use word lists written for this
study; the parse-based component and the MFTE cross-check are the reason we trust the measurement result, and
the hostility null should be read as "six lexical outrage measures operationalised from published categories
show no left-right difference", not as a finding about hostility in general. Ideology and intensity come from a
single language-model instrument, validated by its authors but not, in this corpus, against human coders; that
validation is planned. The survey is a single cross-section without interview dates, so within-show exposure
timing, the one design that could separate influence from selection short of an experiment, is not available in
Year 1. And the number of clusters is small everywhere: 51 to 54 shows carry all of the audience results, which
is why every estimate is reported with small-sample cluster corrections and why we treat degrees of freedom in
the single digits as a warning rather than a technicality.

## What comes next

Year 2 of the survey is pre-registered on this record: the political-content frame fixed in advance from the
published instrument, election distrust as the primary outcome, ideological intensity as the named rival in the
model, and the interview date requested so that exposure can be timed against episodes. Before that, three
checks on the present data: the address measure rebuilt entirely from the published MFTE tagger on the full
corpus, the ten unscored shows brought onto the reference scale, and the within-show test of whether intensity is
partly address in disguise. If the associations reported here survive a confirmatory test with intensity in the
model, the case that how a host talks to an audience matters, independently of what the host says, will have
been made properly. If they do not, the measurement result stands on its own, and the field will have learned
that the register of political podcasting divides left from right more cleanly than its temperature does.
