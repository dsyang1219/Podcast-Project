Partisan media differ in register, not in how they cover the news

Abstract

Partisan political podcasts are separated less by what they say than by the relationship they build with the listener — and that relationship is a fixed property of a show, not a response to events.

Across 219 transcribed shows and 1.38M passages — of which 205 carry the metadata and 194 the ideology scores the show-level tests require — the two sides cover the same news at the same speed with the same tone, and no framing signal transfers across events. Hostility, moral vocabulary and within-episode dynamics are equivalence-tested nulls that do not differ by side. What separates them is habitual register, and the sharpest component of it is address: right-leaning shows issue more commands and more second-person, positioning the listener as someone to be directed; left-leaning shows name officials and hedge, positioning them as someone to be informed (composite d = 0.77, replicated across a dependency parser and a lexical instrument, unmoved by removing ad reads or controlling for format, and confirmed against host campaign contributions with no model in the criterion).

A textual affective-polarization index — in-group warmth minus out-group warmth — survives episode fixed effects, permutation, cluster bootstrapping, a purity check that strengthens it, and a second sentiment instrument, and it tracks ideological intensity (ρ = 0.341). But the questions usually asked of such an index are beyond what 194 shows can resolve: whether the sides differ, and whether polarization has grown, are both withdrawn here as underpowered rather than answered. Moral vocabulary turns out to belong mostly to the subject rather than the speaker — care, loyalty and fairness language varies four to nineteen times more across topics than across sides — and the one apparent exception — authority.vice, right-higher at d = 0.53 — reverses sign on a second moral instrument and is not claimed (§4.4). On published moral-foundations dictionaries, seven of ten foundations carry more vice vocabulary in out-group than in-group passages (§4.3). Whether that asymmetry favours the left or the right depends on Trump, who appears in 48.3% of party-referencing passages — on one foundation, removing him reverses a significant result into the opposite significant result. Topics do not do this — tested against an unsupervised topic model, one of 630 tests survives correction — because an entity must occupy opposite roles for the two sides to distort the comparison at all; among the twelve figures that do, magnitude tracks prominence (r = +0.99), and Biden shifts the same estimates in mirror image.

Change is detectable in one place only. Around the January 2025 transfer of power, with each show as its own control, losing power made the losers more negative about the winners on both sentiment instruments; winning changed nothing about how the winners discuss the losers. Otherwise partisan difference is a trait of shows, not a dynamic within them: register identifies which show is playing at AUC 0.80 and which moment inside it at 0.57.

Sixteen claims made during this analysis were retracted, and the pattern in which ones failed is itself a result — see The argument below. §10 records them all.

The argument
  1. Content does not separate the sides. Same events, same speed, same tone, no transferable framing (§3, §7).
  2. Register does, and address is its sharpest edge. Directive versus expository stance toward the listener, d = 0.77, anchored to donation behaviour, and carried by a dependency parser with no word list at all (§3A). It is the only substantive effect that survives every test.
  3. Affect separates them less than expected. The polarization index is real and validated, but side differences and time trends are below what this design can detect (§4.1–4.2).
  4. Moral vocabulary belongs to the subject, not the speaker. Care, loyalty and fairness language varies 4–19× more across topics than across sides; only authority.vice is a durable partisan signal (§4.4). A single salient actor also moves, and on one foundation reverses, which side appears to moralise more (§4.3).
  5. Difference is a trait, not a response. Nothing inside an episode differs by side; the only detected change follows an external transfer of power, and only for the losers (§4.5, §5, §6).
  6. What survived predicts what will. Counts beat sentiment; levels beat differences; paired within-show designs beat between-show ones. Every retracted claim violated at least one of those (§10).

Identification

Every within-episode estimate is demeaned within episode — algebraically equivalent to episode fixed effects. No difference between shows, hosts, eras or formats can produce a position effect. Verified directly: the full within estimator and the demeaned-outcome estimator differ by 0.007%, because position runs 0.1–0.9 in every episode so its episode-mean is 0.5000 (SD across episodes 0.0022).

Nulls are permuted at the show level, never the passage level — passages within a show are not independent, and passage-level shuffling narrows the null enough to manufacture significance. Standard errors are cluster-robust by show. p-values across measure families carry Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

Scope

The sampling frame is a recent chart, so 217 of 219 shows are still publishing in 2026. The population is political podcasts that attained and held chart presence, not a representative sample. Within-episode comparisons are unaffected; between-show comparisons inherit this survivorship.

Directive register§3A

The right speaks to the listener; the left speaks about the subject

The largest and best-supported difference in this corpus is not about what the two sides discuss. It is about who they address. Right-leaning shows issue more commands and use more second-person address — a register that positions the listener as someone to be directed rather than informed. This is the one finding here that clears every test the affective-polarization claims failed, and it is worth being explicit about why: it is a level rather than a difference of differences, it is measured by counts rather than sentiment, and the effect is large.

Status of this finding

Exploratory, not pre-registered. Polarization§4

A textual measure of affective polarization

Address is one half of a relationship; feeling is the other. This section asks whether the affective half separates the sides too — and finds that the corpus can answer far less about it than the literature routinely assumes.

The out-group pronoun gap in §3 counts references. Affective polarization in the survey literature is not about reference volume — it is in-party warmth minus out-party warmth, the feeling-thermometer gap (Iyengar & Westwood 2015). The textual analog is direct: mean sentiment when a show discusses its own side, minus mean sentiment when it discusses the other.

The same corpus supports opposite significant conclusions about which side moralises more, depending on how much of one man is left in it.

The mechanism is position, not framing

The obvious explanation would be that the two sides moralise Trump differently. They do not. Across all ten foundations the right’s Trump-talk sits within a few percent of the left’s — 1.04× on virtues, 1.03× on vices. What makes him pivotal is that Trump-talk carries a distinctive moral register for everyone, and he occupies opposite roles for the two sides, so that register lands in opposite cells.

Do topics do this too? No — and the reason matters

If salience and moral distinctiveness were sufficient, big issues should be pivotal as well. Tested against the corpus’s fitted LDA topic model (k = 75, unsupervised — no authored word lists), they are not.

A first pass appeared to show they were, and it was wrong in an instructive way. Dropping the 20% of episodes most about each topic and re-estimating suggested immigration was extremely pivotal (authority.vice d = +0.451 → −0.195, a sign flip at 6.9 random-removal null SDs), with Ukraine and the courts close behind. But dropping a topic’s heavy episodes pushes shows below the volume floor: a median of 28 of 150 shows fell out per topic, and the losses were side-skewed differently each time — one topic removed 26 left-leaning shows and 8 right-leaning, another 7 and 17. The apparent pivotality was largely which shows left the sample.

Re-run as a within-show partition — each show’s topic-heavy episodes against its own topic-light episodes, so the show set never changes — the effect disappears. Of 630 topic × foundation tests, one survives Benjamini–Hochberg (religion × authority.vice, d = −0.64). Mean displacement per topic is 0.15–0.18 d, and no topic shifts moral language differently for the two sides.

Role asymmetry is what lets an entity flip a comparison at all. Prominence decides how far.

Immigration, Ukraine and the courts are discussed by both sides as out-group matters, so they load both halves of the comparison the same way and cancel. A partisan figure does not cancel, because they sit on opposite sides of the ledger. Testing that across twelve figures separates the two ingredients cleanly.

The practical rule

Role asymmetry behaves as a threshold rather than a dial. Topics are not free of it — measured the same way, they run 0.17–0.61 (median 0.37) against figures’ 0.57–0.93 — but they sit below where it starts to bite, which is why the topic test returned 1 of 630. Partisan figures all pass the gate, and then their prominence sets the magnitude. A partisan-media comparison is therefore vulnerable in proportion to how concentrated its corpus is on any single partisan figure, and that quantity is reportable: in this corpus one man accounts for 48.3% of all party-referencing passages.

Attribution: what a bag of words can and cannot see

These are bag-of-words counts. A passage is assigned to a side by which party’s terms dominate it, and moral vocabulary anywhere in that passage counts toward that side — so “they lie” and “they say we lie” score identically, negation is invisible, and quoted speech is indistinguishable from the host’s own.

A syntactic version was built to check this: every passage dependency-parsed, with a fairness.vice term counted only when a political actor is its grammatical subject (the nsubj of the verb, or the possessor in “the lies of the Democrats”). It confirms the direction — 1,204 attributed accusations against the out-group against 529 against the in-group, a ratio of 2.28, independent of the attribution problem above.

It cannot do more, and the reason is worth recording. Only 4.0% of fairness.vice tokens have any political actor as their grammatical subject — 1,733 of 43,828. The median show yields five attributable events and only 25 of 194 reach twenty, so no show-level or side comparison is possible on it. That 96% is a general limit on moral-dictionary work in speech: most moral vocabulary in conversation is unattached — it’s a lie, that’s corruption — and no choice of word list recovers a target that the syntax never supplied. Dictionary counts and directed accusation are different constructs, and the results above are the former.

A disgust finding that did not survive

sanctity.vice is the one foundation where the speaker matters more than the subject, and left-leaning shows appeared higher in 72 of 74 topics (sign test p = 2.9e−19) — which would have cut against Moral Foundations Theory’s prediction that conservatives are the purity-sensitive side. It does not hold. Split by year the effect reverses: right-higher in 2020–2023, left-higher only from 2024, and significant only in 2026 (d = −0.43, p = .002). The 97%-of-topics figure was an artifact of corpus composition, since most passages fall in the recent period. It also fails against DIME (r = −0.153, p = .11, non-monotonic).

Decomposing it by term settles what it was. Four swear words carry 88% of the entire left excess: fucking (45.2%), shit (24.5%), fuck (15.5%), fucked (2.8%). No other term exceeds 2%, and the health-policy vocabulary that might plausibly have explained it (addiction, alcoholism, disease) contributes −9.2%, since the right uses drug, drugs and virus more. MFD 2.0’s sanctity.vice list includes profanity — coherent for a degradation construct in edited text, but in transcribed speech it substantially measures swearing. This result was therefore re-measuring the profanity difference already reported in §3 under a moral-theoretical label, and would have contradicted Moral Foundations Theory on the strength of how often hosts say “fucking”. Anyone applying MFD to speech corpora should strip or separately report the profanity terms. By contrast authority.vice is right-higher in every year of the corpus and carries no such contamination.

4.5 What changed when power changed hands

The tests in §4.2 failed because they compared shows to each other. January 2025 supplies something better: a dated transfer of power that no show chose, with every show observable on both sides of it. 152 shows have enough party-referencing material in both the Biden and second-Trump periods to serve as their own controls.

The first result is mechanical but worth stating because it is easy to misread. Out-group attention appears to swing violently — left-leaning shows go from 64.3% to 77.5% of their party talk being about the other side, right-leaning shows from 47.6% down to 35.0%, a difference in shifts of d = 1.97. That is relabelling, not behaviour. Measured on a fixed target rather than relative to each show’s own side, both sides moved toward Republican-talk by almost exactly the same amount: +13.1pp on the left and +12.6pp on the right (d = 0.039, p = .83), in 90% and 86% of shows respectively. Everyone covers the president more; the apparent partisan asymmetry is an artifact of defining “out-group” relative to the speaker.

That equality is itself the paper’s one properly powered symmetry result — TOST certifies equivalence at 0.35 SD (p = .041) — because it is a within-show contrast around a dated shock rather than a comparison of levels across 194 clusters. Left and right podcasts orient to whoever holds power to the same degree.

Why the survey comparison could not be run

Correlating the index against ANES is not possible: ANES is biennial, giving four overlapping points. Nationscape (weekly, July 2019 – February 2021) would give ~80 — but the textual index is not reliable at weekly resolution. Split-half across shows within a week gives r = +0.039 (Spearman–Brown +0.075) and lag-1 autocorrelation +0.097. A week holds a median of 386 passages across 18 shows, where noise (SD 0.107) exceeds signal (mean 0.082). The index has a resolution floor above weekly; any weekly correlation would be uninterpretable.

Why this is a methodological result, not a curiosity

This is the ecological inference problem in a form the design can isolate: a strong between-text association that does not hold within a text. A study comparing shows rather than moments would have concluded the opposite. We committed the same error ourselves mid-analysis — a quarter-level correlation of ρ = −0.607 between news volume and the register shift vanished at episode level (p = 0.88, and p = 0.74 in a split-sample with no shared text).

5.1 Format or trait?

Within-show profile consistency is 0.176 against a shuffled null of 0.096. Above chance, but low. If shows executed a deliberate rundown template each episode, individual episodes would strongly repeat their own show’s shape. They do not. These are population-level tendencies, not per-episode scripts — an episode picked at random may show none of it.

By contrast, political density is a durable show trait: a show’s 2018–2020 density predicts its 2025–2026 density at ρ = +0.725 (p = 6e-14).

5.2 The one audience association

Political density correlates with chart standing: ρ = −0.234 against best rank (p = 0.001), i.e. more political shows chart higher. It holds across every content window — 2018–20 (−0.331), 2021–23 (−0.321), 2024 (−0.323), 2025–26 (−0.200) — so it is not a snapshot artifact. The arc’s steepness shows no association and fails BH correction.

This cannot support causal direction. Both quantities are stable traits, and chart data covers only 12 dates in mid-2026. More political shows may attract audiences, or established shows may invest more in political coverage.

Synthesis§9A

One difference, and the shape of the evidence for it

Everything above converges on a single separation, and it is narrower than the literature usually assumes. Left- and right-leaning shows are not distinguished by the events they cover, the speed at which they reach them, the hostility they bring, the moral vocabulary they use, or anything that happens over the course of an episode. Every one of those was tested and every one came back null or equivalence-tested null. What distinguishes them is a standing posture toward the person listening — directive on one side, expository on the other — and that posture is stable enough to identify a show at AUC 0.80 while identifying a moment inside it at 0.57.

The partisan signal is in the second person, not the third.

One substantive effect survives every test: directive address (§3A). d = 0.77, a null discriminant control, unmoved by format or ad reads, permutation p < .00001, monotonic against contribution records — and, decisively, it does not depend on any word list written for this project. Stripping the authored regex leaves d = 0.744; the spaCy dependency parse alone, which contains no vocabulary at all, gives d = 0.669 with DIME r = +0.360.

Authority-vice (§4.4) does not join it. It passes permutation (p = .0004), leave-one-out, format controls (p = .0095), ad-stripping (p = .0031) and a discriminant control — then fails the second instrument: eMFD’s authority dimension returns d = −0.099 (p = .51) and its sentiment-weighted form d = −0.281, both the opposite sign to MFD 2.0’s +0.520. It is one dictionary’s result. And the two effects are in any case independent: controlling for side, directive register does not predict authority-vice (p = .61), with r ≈ 0 inside each side. Nothing else separates the sides reliably — not hostility, not moral vocabulary, not affect, and nothing inside an episode.

Read that way, the results stop being a list. Directive address (§3A) is the effect itself. The affective-polarization index (§4.1) measures something real but adjacent, and the questions usually asked of it — is it symmetric, is it growing — turn out to be beyond this design (§4.2). Where affect does separate the sides it does so as moral condemnation of the out-group (§4.3) — and which side appears to moralise more turns on how much Trump coverage is left in the sample. The within-episode processes (§5) are real but shared, which is what makes them the control condition rather than the finding. And the one detected change over time (§4.4) arrives from outside, when power changes hands, and moves only the side that lost.

9A.1 Why some claims survived and others did not

Limits§11

What this does not establish

Consequence, as of September 2026

The corpus alone supplied no outcome (chart trajectory, tenure and survival return nothing after correction). Linked to survey data it does: §E reports a population-level association between non-institutional news diets and institutional cynicism in two probability panels, and §F.1 a show-level association between directive register and audience exclusivity. Both are associations under selection; neither is an effect. The original text below is retained.

Neither surviving measure predicts any outcome the corpus can supply. Against share of months charted, ever charting, chart trajectory, charting in 2026, still publishing in 2026, and show tenure, the directive index and authority-vice return twelve tests with nothing surviving correction (best raw p = .06, chart trend). That is a weak test rather than strong evidence of no effect: every show was sampled from a top-250 chart, so the comparison is successful shows against successful shows; the monthly chart panel ends in December 2024; and charting is a coarse proxy for listening. This paper describes how partisan podcasts speak. It does not show that any of it does anything.

  1. No human validation of any LLM label. Neither ideology nor outrage coding has been checked against human coders on this corpus. Test–retest at 92.6% establishes consistency, not correctness. This is the largest outstanding threat and the first thing a replication should address.
  2. Position-dependent scorer bias cannot be excluded. Within-episode demeaning removes bias that is constant for an episode, but not bias that grows with accumulated context. Mitigations — only the preceding passage is supplied as context; dropping early passages leaves the arc intact; the gate does not drift — are suggestive, not decisive.
  3. Not pre-registered, with one exception. The text findings were found in the course of other analyses. The audience half was searched extensively and §G discloses the search in full. One test was pre-registered before the variable was opened — the Q28 free-text test — and it failed on its own criterion (§G.3).
  4. Corpus coverage excludes the largest shows. “Joe Rogan” is named 334 times and “Daily Wire” 75 times by Kettering respondents; neither is in the 204-show corpus, which is mid-tail by construction. The pre-registered out-of-frame test (§F.3.1) has now been run: the address–cynicism association does not extend to the ten largest shows as frozen; two of the ten are non-political by content, and within political-content shows it reappears (§F.3.2). The register results describe political podcasts, defined by content.
  5. Content features are collinear. Address, populist vocabulary and ideological intensity co-vary across shows (r .4–.5) and are jointly associated with audience disaffection; with 50–60 clusters Year 1 ranks them only within the political-content frame (§F.3.4). No interview date exists in Year 1, so within-show time-varying exposure with show fixed effects — the design that would separate effect from selection — is unavailable.
  6. Second-person address is format-sensitive. In host–guest interview formats the measure counts address to the guest. The corpus frame has few such shows and the partisan contrast is robust to the dialogue component (§F.5), but the measure’s level should not be compared across formats without a format covariate or diarization.
  7. The strict title match is the operationalisation. Matching respondents by host name instead of show title adds 159 respondents and has now eliminated four separate results (§G.4). Whether that is a different exposure modality (clip consumption rather than episode listening) or something else is unresolved; every audience-side estimate is reported on the strict match with the expansion as a documented failing sensitivity.
  8. Possibly not political. No within-episode effect interacts with how political a show is. A non-political control probe (346 Lex Fridman episodes) replicated the disfluency rise and found second-person flat — but that show is dyadic, so “you” addresses a guest. A matched non-political corpus is the decisive outstanding test, and it partitions these results into medium effects and political ones.
  9. No causal identification. Every audience-side result is cross-sectional or, in Pew’s case, lagged by 2–8 months — the minimal temporal condition, not identification. People choose their podcasts. The spiral-of-cynicism literature licenses a directional hypothesis and the paper states one; it does not license “causes.” Exposure-weighting with published retention curves changes nothing (listeners hear 58% of words and 58% of political content).
  10. Unit mismatch. Fixed 750-character windows stand in for the diarized speaker turns the ideology instrument was validated on. The 3,581 episodes with archived audio would settle whether it matters.
  11. Small effects, weak per-episode expression. β ≈ 0.13 SD, and within-show profile consistency of 0.176 means an episode picked at random may show none of this. These are population tendencies.

Bivariates§B

Two ideology criteria, and what predicts them

B.1 The ideology score tracks donation behaviour

Everything else in this appendix correlates a register measure against an ideology score the model read off the same transcripts. Those correlations are only worth reading if the score itself tracks something outside the text. DIME cfscores (Bonica 2014) estimate ideology from federal campaign contribution records — a behavioural criterion with no linguistic content at all. 112 of the 194 shows have at least one host matched to a contributor record.

The disagreements are worth separating, because they are not one phenomenon. Some are substantive: The Mona Charen Show and Rational Security are anti-Trump outlets whose hosts carry Republican contribution histories. The measures disagree because the underlying facts disagree — a donation record is a lifetime accumulation and the transcript is the present tense. That is realignment, and the text measure is arguably the more current of the two. Others are plainly criterion error: Life, Liberty & Levin and Wendy Bell Radio are unambiguously right-coded shows assigned liberal cfscores, which is what DIME name-matching does with a common name. Errors of that kind sit in the criterion, not in the text measure, and they push r down — so +0.682 is a floor.

The replication goes further than the score itself. Re-running every bivariate in this appendix against DIME instead of the model’s ideology reproduces the same structure: the two columns of correlations agree at r = +0.903 across the fourteen measures. What predicts ideology is the same whether ideology is measured from speech or from money.

How far these can be pushed

These are bivariate, cross-sectional, and at show level — n = 194, with no controls and no causal content. Several measures are correlated with each other, so the individual coefficients are not independent contributions. And ideological intensity is itself an LLM-derived quantity, so its correlates inherit whatever the instrument gets wrong. They describe the shape of the corpus; they do not identify anything.

The corpus carries no host gender variable. E.2a A full profile: podcast-only diets are engaged anti-institutionalism, not disengagement

Profiling every usable survey item (115) against the diet segments with full controls and Benjamini–Hochberg across the family: 46 items separate podcast-only diets from mainstream-only. The pooled “division of labour” (address→grievance, populism→cynicism) is produced by the out-of-frame mega-shows and is not a corpus finding. F.5.2’s claim that populism is ruled out as a mechanism is therefore retracted (§10): it was true of the 385-listener discovery sample with the 4-item composite and is not true of the pooled data with the 8-item composite. F.4 The party-favourability gap, demoted

The original audience-side headline was directive exposure against the |Republican − Democratic| favourability gap: b = +0.59, t = 2.94 on the strict 384. Under the wild cluster bootstrap that is p = .04, and under CR2 with Satterthwaite df = 8.4 it is p = .015: the coefficient is identified from about eight effective clusters, not fifty-one, because 24 of 51 clusters have one respondent. Precision-weighted at the show level it is b ≈ +0.43 to +0.53 with permutation p between .02 and .07 across thresholds. The host-name expansion takes it to +0.10 (p = .35), and one show — Letters from an American, directive score −2.80 against a corpus SD of 0.82, 55 listeners with an above-average gap — flattens the slope alone. Popularity and platform controls do not move it; the matching rule does. A marginal, specification-dependent association: at most a hedged secondary result.

F.5 What “directive” is, and three content mechanisms it is not

The composite averages syntactic imperatives, dictionary imperatives and second-person pronouns, and each is ambiguous in speech. Decomposing it on a 122,444-passage stratified sample with a dependency parser: the imperatives are mostly action verbs (45%: go, get, make, take, do) and “let” (13%: let me tell you); only 6% are epistemic (understand, realize, remember). Of second-person tokens, 19% are the filler you know, 7% deontic (you need to, you should, you have to), 72% generic. Against host ideology (DIME): filler you know is r = 0.00; deontic you is +0.44, the strongest single subtype; generic you +0.38; action imperatives +0.37; epistemic imperatives +0.15 (ns). MFTE’s own imperative and second-person tags agree with the parser (r = +0.51, +0.43 with the composite). But Biber’s Dimension 4, overt expression of persuasion — necessity modals, prediction modals, conditionals, split auxiliaries — is uncorrelated with the composite (r = −0.12) and necessity modals alone are null everywhere. Deontic you carries the signal and the modal does not: it is the second-person subject, not the modality. The construct is listener-directed address — the host speaking to the audience, with action imperatives — not conversational informality, not epistemic instruction, not persuasion in Biber’s sense. It is also event-invariant: within show, it does not move around the 2024 election (−0.03 SD, placebo-date p = .57 against 60 random dates), the inauguration, the assassination attempt, Biden’s withdrawal, the 2022 and 2020 elections, January 6 or Dobbs — all |b| ≤ 0.08 SD, no side interaction, no pre-election run-up. Nor does it move with topic: across a 75-topic LDA, within-show topic means spread only 0.04 SD against 0.23 across shows, topic adds 0.005 to R² over show, and the most second-person “topics” are ad reads and sign-offs, not politics. It is a trait of the host, not a reaction to the calendar or the subject, which is what the between-show/within-show dissociation (§6) already implied and what a para-social reading predicts. One measurement limit surfaced by the out-of-frame test (§H): in long-form interview shows the second person is largely addressed to a guest, and the instrument cannot tell that from address to the listener — The Joe Rogan Experience scores +0.86 on the corpus scale for that reason, and sentence-type heuristics do not fix it (Rogan’s “you” is 69% declarative). The corpus is monologue and co-host dominated, so this bites the level of the measure, not its partisan contrast: the interrogative component of second-person address is unrelated to host ideology (r = +0.03 against DIME) and partialling it out leaves the DIME validation unchanged (r = 0.49 vs 0.47). Position in the episode carries a text-only format fingerprint (monologue shows concentrate address in the opening; interview shows are flat) and is pre-registered as a sensitivity for the holdout; diarization is the clean fix. Among corpus listeners the composite outperforms any single subtype on cynicism (+0.20 vs +0.10 to +0.15; subtypes come from a sample and are noisier); on exclusivity deontic you is the cleanest carrier (+0.11 per SD, CR2 p = .025, wild .023).

F.5.1 Not horse-race journalism — and game framing runs the other way

The spiral-of-cynicism mechanism (Cappella & Jamieson 1997) operates through strategy and game framing — politics as who is winning, polls, tactics. If directive shows were also the game-frame shows, that established mechanism would absorb the mode-of-address result. A lexicon after Aalberg, Strömbäck & de Vreese (2012), with horse-race and strategy subframes scored separately over all 1.37 million passages, says they are different things.

F.5.2 Not populist anti-elitism, not anti-media populism — on validated instruments

The Rooduijn & Pauwels (2011) populism dictionary, scored over all 1.37 million passages, is orthogonal to listener-directed address (r = +0.11, ns) and not partisan (r = −0.04 against DIME); it tracks insult instead (r = +0.41). People-centrism and negative media references (anti-media populism after Fawzi 2019) behave the same way (r = +0.01 with address; +0.22 and +0.38 with insult). G.3 Three tests were pre-registered; all three failed

The out-of-frame generalization test and the within-frame fresh-listener test (both 3 September 2026, §F.3.1) join the Q28 free-text test below. Each had hypothesis, data, model, inference and failure criterion frozen to a hashed file before its data were examined, and each failed on its own criterion. The paper reports all three. Everything in §F.3.2–F.3.4 was found afterward and is labelled exploratory.

The survey’s free-text item Q28 — “What does democracy mean to you?”, 20,280 responses, median 14 words — had never been examined against register. A hypothesis, four dictionary measures, controls, inference and a failure criterion were written to a timestamped file before the variable was opened; the analysis ran two seconds later. The primary measure (out-group pronoun share) returned +0.12 SD, one-sided p = .125: fail by the pre-stated criterion. Negation rate +0.13 (p = .097) and a cynicism lexicon +0.13 (p = .062) pointed the same way; valence did not. Directionally consistent at about half the Likert-scale effect, which is what a noisier measure of the same construct would give; not a confirmation, and reported as a failed one.

G.4 The matching rule is a researcher degree of freedom

Matching respondents by host name rather than show title (“benny johnson” → The Benny Show) is defensible and adds 159 respondents. It has now removed four results: the favourability gap (p .0035 → .35), election distrust, listener cynicism (.0027 → .165), and exclusivity (.0085 → .17). One hypothesis is that people who write a host’s name rather than a show title are consuming clips on video platforms rather than episodes, a different exposure modality from the full transcripts the register measure is built on. That is a hypothesis. The fact is that “who counts as exposed” was not fixed in advance, so every estimate is reported on the strict rule and the expansion is reported as a sensitivity that fails.
