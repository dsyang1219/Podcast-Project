Paper précis · September 2026

Talking at the Audience

Listener-directed address in political podcasts, and the institutional cynicism of the people who listen
Danielle Yang · Linde Center for Science, Society, and Policy, Caltech · with R. Michael Alvarez. This page holds only the data, theory, literature and results that belong in the paper. The full exploratory record, including everything retracted, is the companion document Partisan Register.
Abstract

We assemble a corpus of 204 U.S. political podcasts from the Apple Podcasts Politics chart (29,421 episodes, 1.38 million 750-character passages, 2018–2026) and measure how hosts address their listeners using published dictionaries and syntactic parsing. Show ideology labels from a two-stage LLM classifier agree with hosts’ campaign-contribution scores (DIME, r = 0.68, n = 112), with expert and editorial ratings (κ = 0.75–0.77), and with the party identification of each show’s own listeners in a national survey (r = 0.91 across 22 shows; the label predicts the audience’s majority party in 21 of 22). The register features most associated with ideology are imperatives (r = 0.39 against DIME) and second-person address (r = 0.39); insult is uncorrelated with ideological direction (r = 0.00). Register is a stable show-level property (AUC 0.80 between shows, 0.57 within). Linking the corpus to the Kettering–Gallup Democracy for All study (n ≈ 18,800) and the Pew American Trends Panel (n ≈ 9,000), respondents who name a political podcast as a news source score 0.22 SD higher on an eight-item institutional-cynicism scale than other news consumers, after controls for party, political attention, age, education, income, race, gender, social-media use, urbanicity and number of sources named; each mainstream source named is associated with −0.10 SD; podcast-only diets exceed platform-only diets by 0.14 SD. The association holds within both parties and in the probability-sample half, and replicates in Pew with exposure measured two to eight months before the outcome, with trust in friends and family unaffected. Decomposition and a standard register tagger identify the construct as listener-directed address — second-person and action imperatives, not filler, epistemic instruction, or persuasion in Biber’s sense — and it is invariant to events and topics: a trait of the host. The population is political podcasts, defined by content (at least 15% of passages political; the corpus 10th percentile). On that frame — 50 corpus shows and the six largest political podcasts outside the chart, 792 listeners — address is associated with election distrust (+0.27 SD per SD), institutional cynicism (+0.22) and disaffection (+0.16), inside and outside the corpus, stable across the content threshold and every leave-one-show-out, and net of populist and ideologically intense content; the association is reported as exploratory and pre-registered for Year 2. Independently of attitudes, address predicts who listens: audiences of listener-directed shows have less formal education (−0.27 SD per SD; income, age and party controlled), replicating in every sample. Podcast-only listeners are not disengaged: they are more civil-libertarian on democratic norms than mainstream-only news consumers (e.g. −0.35 SD on media taking government direction), no more strongly partisan, and no less registered, whereas platform-only listeners show weaker national identity, lower registration and more tolerance of political violence. Within the corpus, listener-directed address also predicts naming no institutional news source (0.13 SD per SD; p = .025 with small-sample clustered inference), independent of show ideology, while hostility does not; that association does not reproduce out of frame and is reported as corpus-only.

Argument1

What the paper claims

How the sides differ. Political podcasts are a major and under-measured political medium. The intuitive account of how left and right differ in it — that right-leaning shows are more hostile — is not what the data show. What distinguishes the sides is how hosts address their listeners — listener-directed address: right-leaning hosts speak to the audience in the second person and issue more instructions. Incivility does not discriminate, and neither does populist or anti-media content. That register is a property of shows, not episodes; it does not move with elections or topics; and the ideology labels it rides on are validated against three independent sources, including the party identification of actual listeners.

Who listens to political podcasts. People who get political news from podcasts — and especially from podcasts alone — are markedly more cynical about institutions than people with any institutional source in their diet, in two probability panels, one with exposure measured before outcome, independent of party and of education, with interpersonal trust unaffected. Podcast-only diets sit at the far end of a broader non-institutional-diet gradient. These listeners are not disengaged but civil-libertarian: distrustful of institutions, protective of liberties, no more partisan than anyone else.

What address does within political podcasts. Defining the population by content — shows that are actually about politics, inside the corpus and the largest ones outside it — listener-directed address predicts two things about a show’s audience. First, who listens: audiences of listener-directed shows have less formal education, with income, age and party held constant, in every sample. Second, what they believe: they are more distrustful of elections and of institutions, and more disaffected, inside and outside the corpus, net of populist and ideologically intense content. The first is a register–audience fit result and is robust. The second is an association the paper reports as exploratory — its population definition was fixed after the pre-registered test — and pre-registers for Year 2.

Partisan difference in political podcasting lies in how hosts address their audience, not how hostile they are; the audiences of non-institutional political media are distinctively cynical about institutions; and within political podcasts, the hosts who talk at the listener reach less-educated audiences who distrust elections and institutions.

Every audience-side result is an association under self-selection and is never called an effect. The theoretical frame (below) licenses a directional hypothesis the results are consistent with; it does not license causation, and the paper says so.

Theory2

Theory and literature

2.1 The outcome: media malaise and the spiral of cynicism

The claim that exposure to a media form erodes trust in institutions has a long lineage. Robinson (1976) named videomalaise for television; Cappella and Jamieson (1997) showed experimentally that strategy- and game-framed coverage of politics produces cynicism about the process, a spiral in which cynical coverage and cynical audiences reinforce one another; Ladd (2012) showed that distrust of the news media itself is politically consequential and travels with distrust of other institutions. This literature is the anchor for the outcome: it predicts that a news diet built outside institutional journalism should go with lower institutional trust, and gives that prediction a direction. The mechanism those authors identified concerns content frames, and the paper tests it directly: a strategy-and-game-frame lexicon after Aalberg, Strömbäck and de Vreese (2012), scored over the full corpus, is uncorrelated with listener-directed address at the show level (r = −0.04), not partisan (r = −0.002 against DIME), and — in this population — associated with less listener cynicism, not more. Mode of address and strategy framing are different things.

2.2 The mechanism this paper proposes: listener-directed address

The register measure is not about frames. It is about the grammatical relationship a host establishes with the listener — imperatives (“look,” “understand,” “think about it”), second-person address, and in-group/out-group pronoun framing — drawn from Biber’s (1988) register dimensions and measured with the Multi-Feature Tagger of English and syntactic parsing rather than lists written for this study. The closest prior work is Horton and Wohl’s (1956) para-social interaction: direct, personal address that invites the audience into a one-sided relationship with a performer. What the measure captures, on decomposition, is the host speaking to the listener: deontic second person (you need to, you should) is the strongest single component against host ideology (r = +0.44), the filler you know is null (r = 0.00), epistemic imperatives are null, and Biber’s Dimension 4 “overt persuasion” — necessity and prediction modals, conditionals — is uncorrelated with it (r = −0.12). The second-person subject carries the signal, not the modality. The paper therefore names the construct listener-directed address, proposes it as the linguistic form of the para-social relationship in political podcasting, offers the audience-side signature (finding 5) as evidence, and tests its association with listener attitudes against three content-based alternatives — strategy framing (Aalberg et al.), populist anti-elitism (Rooduijn & Pauwels 2011), and anti-media populism (Fawzi 2019). In the discovery sample the address–cynicism association survived all three (+0.19 SD per SD). On the full political-podcast frame, net of populist and ideologically intense content, what remains is an association with election distrust and institutional cynicism (finding 7); what holds in every sample is that address determines who listens (finding 3).

2.3 What the paper rules out — incivility — and what it does not: populist content

Mutz and Reeves (2005) showed that televised incivility lowers trust in government; Sobieraj and Berry (2011) documented the outrage register of partisan talk. The obvious extension is that podcast hostility drives podcast audiences’ cynicism. The data do not support it: insult and profanity do not distinguish left-leaning from right-leaning shows (finding 2), do not predict who listens (finding 3), and do not predict audience exclusivity (finding 6) or the audience attitudes in finding 7. Populist content behaves the same way on the partisan question: the Rooduijn & Pauwels dictionary and negative references to the media are orthogonal to listener-directed address (r = +0.16 across the 64 linked shows), not partisan, and load on insult (r = +0.41 and +0.38) — they belong to the hostility cluster. Whatever the outrage and populism literatures explain, it is not the partisan structure of this medium. On the audience question the paper is more careful: across all 64 linked shows, populist vocabulary (corrupt, propaganda, betrayed, the truth) and ideological intensity are each associated with listener cynicism, the three content features inter-correlate at 0.4–0.5, and with 50–60 show clusters Year 1 can rank them only on the full political-podcast frame (finding 7). The paper says so rather than ruling populism out.

2.4 Affective polarization

Iyengar and Westwood (2015) define affective polarization as the gap between in-party and out-party feeling. The corpus carries a textual analogue — in-group versus out-group valence, validated within episode — and the survey carries party favourability ratings. The paper uses the survey measure only as a secondary outcome; institutional cynicism is where the association is robust.

Median episode 54 minutes. The sampling frame is a recent chart, so the population is political podcasts that attained and held chart presence in the Politics subcategory. That is mid-tail by construction: the largest political shows in the ecosystem — The Ben Shapiro Show, The Daily, The Tucker Carlson Show — are filed under other Apple categories and are not in the corpus. The paper therefore defines its population by content rather than by chart: a political podcast is one in which at least 15% of 750-character passages contain three or more political terms, the corpus’s own 10th percentile (median 32%). Four low-content corpus shows fall outside it, and the six largest political podcasts never on the chart fall inside it and serve as an out-of-frame generalization test (finding 7).

3.2 Register instruments

Four non-overlapping families, all published before this study: Biber features via the Multi-Feature Tagger of English (Le Foll & Shakir); syntactic imperative detection with spaCy dependency parsing (a bare-form verb heading a clause with no subject or auxiliary); the extended Moral Foundations Dictionary (Hopp et al. 2021) and MFD 2.0 (Frimer et al.); and Sobieraj and Berry’s outrage categories with the LDNOOBW profanity list. The listener-directed-address composite is the mean of three show-level z-scores: syntactic imperatives, dictionary imperatives, and second-person pronouns, each an n-weighted per-10,000-word rate. One boundary on the measure: in host–guest interview formats the second person is largely addressed to the guest. The corpus is monologue and co-host dominated, and the partisan contrast is robust to this — the interrogative component of second-person address is unrelated to host ideology (r = +0.03 against DIME) and partialling it out leaves the validation unchanged (0.49 vs 0.47) — but the measure’s level is not comparable across formats without a format covariate; the out-of-frame test carries one (finding 7).

    Pre-registration and disclosure. The out-of-frame test was pre-registered before any audio was fetched — hypothesis, frozen alias table, eight-item outcome, model, inference, failure criterion, hashed — and it was pre-registered on a wider set of shows selected by chart absence rather than by content. On that wider set it did not pass; the content criterion that defines the paper’s population was adopted afterward. It is symmetric, objective and insensitive to its threshold, but it is post hoc, and the association above is reported as exploratory for that reason. The full pre-registered result is in the companion record. Kettering Year 2 pre-registers the content frame in advance, with election distrust as the primary outcome for address, populist vocabulary and intensity as covariates, and interview dates requested so that within-show variation in address can be used with show fixed effects.

Inference5

Design and inference

Every within-corpus estimate is demeaned within episode; every between-show estimate is permuted at the show level and carries Benjamini–Hochberg correction across measure families. Survey estimates are weighted with the surveys’ own weights and use HC1 standard errors where exposure is respondent-level.

Where exposure is assigned by show, respondents cluster in a small number of large shows: on the 56-show political-podcast frame, 57% of clusters hold fewer than five listeners and the four largest (MeidasTouch, Shapiro, NBC Nightly News, The Daily) hold 47%. Conventional cluster-robust inference is unreliable in that regime (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2008). Every show-clustered estimate therefore reports CR2 with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (Pustejovsky & Tipton 2018) as the primary p and a wild cluster bootstrap alongside; the Satterthwaite df — 7 to 8 on the discovery sample, about 9 on the full political-podcast frame — is the honest count of clusters the coefficient is identified from. The two methods agree on every estimate.

Corpus respondents are matched to shows by exact title (with a documented re-scan for misspellings and abbreviations); out-of-frame respondents by an alias table frozen before any outcome was opened. A host-name expansion of the corpus match is reported once as a sensitivity and does not reproduce the show-linked results; the paper treats the title match as the operationalisation and states the discrepancy.

The out-of-frame test was pre-registered before its data existed — hypothesis, frozen alias table, outcome, model, inference and failure criterion written to hashed files, with amendments filed before any outcome was opened. The content definition of the population came after it (finding 7), so the audience associations of address are labelled exploratory; the education-composition result and the comparison with populist vocabulary and ideological intensity were likewise found by searching and are labelled so. Because the three content features inter-correlate at 0.4–0.5 and identification rests on about 56 show clusters, the paper does not claim to have adjudicated among them beyond what finding 7 reports.

Limits6

Scope and limitations

  • Association, not effect. People choose their podcasts. The Pew lag establishes temporal order, not identification. The paper states a directional hypothesis grounded in the media-malaise literature and reports results consistent with it.
  • Scope. The corpus is the Politics subcategory chart; the largest political podcasts are absent by construction, and the six that meet the content criterion are used as the out-of-frame test. The population is political podcasts defined by content, not by chart.
  • The content definition of the population is post hoc. It was adopted after the pre-registered out-of-frame test, which used a chart-based set, did not pass (finding 7). It is stated as such and is pre-registered for Year 2.
  • Content features are collinear. Address, populist vocabulary and ideological intensity co-vary across shows and are jointly associated with audience disaffection; with about 56 clusters Year 1 ranks them only as far as finding 7 reports. Address’s clearest audience correlate, education composition, does not depend on the ranking.
  • Passage-level validation. Show labels are validated four ways; individual passage labels have not been checked against human coders. A 200-passage, two-coder check precedes submission.
  • Small effective cluster counts on the show-linked results, reported honestly through the Satterthwaite df.
  • Address is format-sensitive. Interview-format shows score high on second person for the wrong reason. The corpus frame contains few; the partisan contrast survives the dialogue component; cross-format comparison of levels needs a format covariate or diarization.
