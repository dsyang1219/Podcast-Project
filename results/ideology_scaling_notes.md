# Anchoring and scaling an LLM ideology measure: what the literature does

*Research notes, 28 November 2026. Written for the rebuild of the ideology / one-sidedness measure. Sources are linked; numbers come from the papers themselves unless marked "reported by".*

## 1. The problem, precisely

The Much et al. (2026) rubric we transcribed (`step4_ideology/prompts/ideology.md`) defines seven labels by **direction × emphasis**: direction from two cue lists (mainstream party positions), steps by cue count ("slightly: mild or single cue; moderately: multiple cues; strongly: dominant, emphatic stance"). It therefore has no notion of ideological extremity and no endpoints, and it has nothing pinning any step to a reference outside the prompt, so the same label can mean different things across models, prompts and runs. Two distinct problems: a **construct** problem (emphasis is not position) and a **calibration** problem (differential item functioning, DIF, across raters, where the raters are LLM configurations).

For the paper as written this is a naming matter: direction (validated at the show level: Brookings κ .83, DIME r .68, listeners' party r .92) and one-sidedness (emphasis) are what the analyses use. For a rebuild that wants a position measure, the literature below is the toolbox.

## 2. Pre-LLM text scaling: three ideas we inherit

- **Reference texts define the scale.** Wordscores (Laver, Benoit and Garry 2003) positions "virgin" texts by word-frequency similarity to reference texts with *assigned* scores, so the ends of the scale are whatever reference texts the researcher supplies (typically party manifestos with expert scores). Wordfish (Slapin and Proksch 2008) estimates a latent dimension without references and then needs two texts fixed to identify direction and scale. Validation work finds both reliable only in narrow registers (poor on social media; Wordscores drifting from expert placements), which is the standard motivation for LLM approaches. [Overview and comparisons in Le Mens and Gallego 2025 and Burnham 2024.]
- **Comparative judgment beats absolute rating for people.** Carlson and Montgomery (2017, *APSR* 111: 835–843) have crowdworkers compare pairs of texts (Senate campaign ads for negativity; State Department human-rights reports) and scale them with a Bradley–Terry model, arguing the task is faster, needs no codebook training, and is more reliable than asking workers for absolute scores. The pairwise logic goes back to Thurstone's law of comparative judgment (1927). [Carlson and Montgomery abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/pairwise-comparison-framework-for-fast-flexible-and-reliable-human-coding-of-political-texts/017BF6B024228962FDF90B47FD90EF5F)
- **Anchors correct scale-use differences across raters.** Aldrich and McKelvey (1977) assume every respondent perceives the same stimuli (politicians) and estimate each respondent's own stretch and shift of the 7-point scale from their ratings of those common stimuli; Hare et al. (2015) give the Bayesian version. King, Murray, Salomon and Tandon (2004, *APSR*) generalise this with **anchoring vignettes**: fixed descriptions rated by everyone, used to put all raters on one scale. Jessee (2020, *Political Analysis*, "Estimating Individuals' Political Perceptions While Adjusting for Differential Item Functioning") combines the two for the 7-point liberal–conservative scale and finds that most disagreement in ideology ratings is perception, not scale use. For us the "respondents" are LLM configurations, and the method transfers unchanged: rate a fixed anchor set in every batch, estimate each configuration's stretch and shift, correct. [Jessee 2020](https://laits.utexas.edu/~sjessee/research-files/Ideo7Vignettes.pdf)

## 3. LLM-era scaling: what has been shown

**Pairwise comparison with Bradley–Terry ("LaMP" scores).** Wu, Nagler, Tucker and Messing (2023) ask ChatGPT which of two senators is more conservative, fit Bradley–Terry, and get a scale correlating about .97 with DW-NOMINATE for the 116th Senate; issue-specific scales (gun control, abortion) place pro-choice Republicans among Democrats and predict interest-group ratings and votes better than ideology alone. Follow-ups apply the same design to federal agencies (7,503 comparisons, 2025) and to Japanese parliamentarians (KOKKAI DOC, 2025), and DiGiuseppe and Flynn (reported by a 2026 replication paper) find pairwise scales agree across models more closely than 0–10 point ratings. [Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12057) · [agencies](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06540) · [KOKKAI DOC](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07118v1) · [replication paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06625)

**Pairwise comparison of texts, with a concept breakdown first.** Wu, Nagler, Tucker and Messing (2025, "Concept-Guided Chain-of-Thought") scale 500 tweets for aversion to each party: a fixed codebook-like sequence of prompts produces a "concept-specific breakdown" of each text, the model then compares breakdowns pairwise (ties allowed, answer extracted in a second call), 20 matchups per text (10,000 of 124,750 possible pairs), Bradley–Terry with bias-reduced ML (BradleyTerry2) and quasi-standard errors. Spearman with human coder counts rises from .55/.56 (raw-text pairwise) to .64/.61 (breakdown pairwise); Wordfish on the same texts is .03/.04. Scores from 5, 10, 15 and 20 matchups per item correlate above .90, so 20 is ample. Not yet analysed: run-to-run and cross-model stability. [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12049)

**Asking and averaging.** Le Mens and Gallego (2025, *Political Analysis*, Letter) ask the model where a *text* (a tweet, a speech, a manifesto) stands on a dimension, then average over many texts to position an actor; correlations with expert coding, crowd coding or roll calls exceed .90 (GPT-4 on British manifestos vs expert placement: .93, .91, .88 across dimensions), better than supervised classifiers trained on large labelled sets. The scale is still verbal at the ends, so this is a noise-reduction method, not an anchoring method. [paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-analysis/article/positioning-political-texts-with-large-language-models-by-asking-and-averaging/BB17F58E7329B53BC4B5F4065C7E00FF)

**Direct elicitation checked against NOMINATE.** "Measurement in the Age of LLMs" (2023) elicits legislator ideal points from gpt-3.5-turbo and reports r = .97 with DW-NOMINATE, with the caveat that most of that is the party split. Linear-probe work (ICLR 2025) predicts NOMINATE from model activations. Every paper in this group warns that agreement with NOMINATE, itself a roll-call measure, is not evidence of capturing ideology in general. [arXiv 2312.09203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.09203) · [ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1557cc3e1a67f985dbdb162db9963dd0-Paper-Conference.pdf)

**Stance battery plus IRT ("Semantic Scaling").** Burnham (2024) has the LLM classify each document's stance on a set of researcher-defined items, turning text into survey-like responses, and fits a Bayesian item-response model to get ideal points. On Twitter, where it disagrees with Tweetscores, human labellers side with it; in Congress it recovers first-dimension NOMINATE. This is the only approach in which position is built from stances rather than inferred from tone, and the researcher chooses the dimension. [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02472)

**Pairwise versus pointwise, head to head.** Licht, Sarkar, Wu, Goel, Stoehr, Ash and Hoyle (EMNLP 2025, "Measuring Scalar Constructs in Social Science with LLMs") re-analyse Carlson and Montgomery's ad-negativity and immigration-fear data plus Park's grandstanding data. Findings that matter here: (i) modal 1–9 scores "heap" on a few values; (ii) the **probability-weighted mean over scale tokens** fixes most of that and is as good as or better than pairwise + Bradley–Terry in Spearman terms (Qwen-72B on ad negativity: pointwise .91–.92 vs pairwise .87), with pairwise often winning on RMSE; (iii) anchored scale-point exemplars "did not consistently improve metrics over 5-shot" random exemplars; (iv) pair-order bias must be averaged out (four orderings); (v) fine-tuning a small reward model on 1,000–2,000 human pairs matches or beats prompting; (vi) closed APIs did not return usable log-probabilities, so weighted pointwise needs an open model. [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.03116v2)

**The LLM-evaluation literature** (QuRating, PairS) reports that models are more self-consistent and better aligned with humans when comparing than when scoring, which is the folk justification for pairwise; the human-annotation evidence is mixed. [PairS](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.16950v4) · [QuRating](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09739)

## 4. What this implies for our measure

1. **A relative scale is the norm, and endpoints are supplied by reference units, not by words.** Wordscores does it with reference manifestos; the LLM pairwise papers do it by regressing the latent scale on NOMINATE. Writing endpoint definitions into a prompt is the one approach nobody validates, and Licht et al. find exemplar anchors do not reliably help.
2. **For an API model without log-probabilities, pairwise + Bradley–Terry is the validated route**; with an open model (Qwen-2.5-72B on the lab GPU), probability-weighted pointwise scoring is as accurate at a fraction of the comparisons. Both are better than modal 7-point labels, which heap.
3. **Compare breakdowns, not raw passages.** The CGCoT result (.55 → .64) says a fixed "what positions does this speaker take" step before the comparison removes a lot of noise; it also makes the comparison about position rather than tone.
4. **Twenty matchups per unit is enough**; four pair orderings per matchup; Bradley–Terry with quasi-standard errors; ties allowed.
5. **DIF correction across models and runs is a solved survey problem.** Rate a fixed anchor set with every configuration and apply Aldrich–McKelvey / anchoring-vignette adjustment. This is the cheapest thing on the list and the only one that gives a running check on instrument drift.
6. **If position (not emphasis) is the construct, build it from stances** (Semantic Scaling) or from pairwise position comparisons; the current rubric cannot be re-anchored into a position measure because its steps are cue counts.

## 5. A design for this corpus

- **Units:** the 219 shows, plus reference units: Senate floor-speech excerpts from senators spanning DW-NOMINATE (public, time-matched), the 2024 and earlier party platforms, and the three in-corpus shows hosted by former legislators with NOMINATE scores (Cruz, Franken, Gingrich) as an in-register check.
- **Items per unit:** 40 political passages per show (already identified by stage 1), 40 speech excerpts per reference senator.
- **Step 1, breakdown:** one fixed prompt per passage extracting the policy positions the speaker endorses or opposes (the CGCoT idea; also the raw material for a stance battery later).
- **Step 2, comparison:** "Whose policy positions are further to the right?" on pairs of breakdowns drawn from different units, topic-matched where possible, four orderings, ties allowed; about 20 matchups per unit → roughly 6,000 comparisons for 219 shows plus references.
- **Step 3, scale:** Bradley–Terry (bias-reduced), quasi-SEs; regress the latent scores of the reference senators on NOMINATE to express the scale in NOMINATE units; report platforms' positions on it.
- **Step 4, calibration:** a 12-passage anchor set rated by every configuration (model, prompt version, run) and used for Aldrich–McKelvey-style correction of any pointwise outputs still in use.
- **Evaluation:** test–retest across runs (ICC); cross-model agreement (Much report r .83 at the show level); monotonicity of win probability against the current 7-point gap on 300 held-out pairs; convergent validity against Brookings, DIME and listeners' party (must not fall below the current κ .83 / r .68 / r .92); discriminant validity by regressing scores on passage length and Comparative Agendas topic; human agreement on the planned 120 passages with half held out from any anchor or exemplar choice; predictive validity in the audience models last.
